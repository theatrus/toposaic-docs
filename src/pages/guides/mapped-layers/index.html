Guide

Choosing what the map draws.

A ski map wants lifts and no trains. A city map wants trains and no lifts. An island map wants the ferry. The Surface tab decides which mapped features reach the model — and because filament slots are handed out only to layers that draw something, switching one off is never wasted, and switching one on costs nothing where the map has none.

What each layer covers

Every layer below has its own map download and its own cache, so switching one never re-fetches the others.

LayerWhat it drawsDefault color
Roads Streets at your chosen detail level. Where no road crosses the area, paths and trails stand in. Route
Railways Heavy rail, light rail, metros, trams, narrow gauge, funiculars, monorails, and miniature and preserved lines. Steel blue-grey
Aerial lifts Cable cars, gondolas, mixed lifts, chair lifts, drag lifts, T-bars, platters, rope tows, and magic carpets. Signal violet
Ferries Every crossing OpenStreetMap tags route=ferry, raised across the water like a road. Deep teal
Airport surfaces Runways, airstrips, stopways, taxiways, taxilanes, aprons, and helipads. Off to start. Slate grey
Trails Your own GPX and KML routes, one line per track. High-vis magenta
Water Rivers, streams, canals, and mapped water areas, flush with the terrain. Water
Buildings OpenStreetMap footprints raised above the ground, with straight mapped edges. Building

Line width follows the kind of line: a mainline railway gets a full-width formation, a rope tow gets a cable. Ferries carry none of that machinery — a crossing has no gauge — so they draw at one width.

Nothing is reserved. A layer only asks for a filament when the mapped area really contains that feature. A city with no cable cars is never asked for a cable-car spool; an inland map never spends one on ferries. The same goes for the base colors — a wilderness map with no water, roads, or buildings asks for none of the three.

Airport surfaces

Airports are the one layer that starts switched off. An airport is something you go looking for, and a map that happens to clip the edge of one should not sprout a runway.

Four groups switch on and off on their own — runways and airstrips, taxiways and taxilanes, aprons, helipads — and all four share one color and one filament slot. They are one printed surface to the eye and one filament in the slicer, so splitting them would spend spools on a distinction nobody asked for.

Pavement is graded, not draped. A real runway is built to a steady gradient and is level across its width. Following the elevation data per point gives neither — the strip corrugates along its length and tilts side to side wherever two coarse samples disagree. So the ground is read once along the centre line, smoothed enough to lose the noise while keeping the real slope, and the whole width of the strip sits at its own station's height.

OpenStreetMap often carries the same pavement twice: an outline around it and a centre line down it. Drawing both would lay the surface down twice, once at its true shape and once as a ribbon of guessed width, so where an outline covers a line the line is dropped. Outlines keep their holes, so an apron drawn around a terminal stays apron everywhere except where the terminal stands — and the terminal itself still prints in the building color.

Two settings are worth knowing:

  • Maximum airport width — a 60 m runway on a close view is a correct reading of the data and still wider than anyone wants across the model.
  • Small feature cutoff — the ground span past which helipads are dropped, before they are requested rather than after. A whole airport graph at an eighty-kilometre view is a mass of unprintable threads.

The airport boundary itself is never drawn. aeroway=aerodrome covers the grass, the car parks, and the fields, so painting it would put pavement over most of a map that merely contains an airport.

Folding two layers into one color

When your printer has fewer spools than the map has features, a layer can borrow another layer's color instead of switching off. Each toggle carries its own style picker.

  • Own color — the default everywhere. The color swatch and width slider appear under it.
  • Draw with roads — available to railways, lifts, ferries, and airport surfaces. The layer prints in the route color at the route width, so it still shows, and costs no extra filament.
  • Draw with railways — lifts only. Lifts and trains share one color. With the railway layer switched off, this falls back to the road color rather than making an enabled layer vanish.

The 3D legend names whatever the model actually shows. A layer drawn in its own color gets its own entry; a layer that borrowed one is named by the entry it borrowed — so lifts following separately colored railways appear under Rail, and anything drawn with roads appears under Route.

Sharing a color is not the same as sharing a slot. Folding a layer in is a Surface-tab decision about what the model draws. Two separate layers set to the same hex value in the Colors tab also share one filament — see the color printing guide. Either route saves a spool.

Bridges, tunnels, and lines out of use

Roads and railways tagged as bridges get a deck interpolated between their end heights, so a viaduct spans the ravine instead of dropping into it. Tunnels vanish, as they should — which takes most metros with them. Untagged roads follow the terrain, and OpenStreetMap's layer=* is not read as a height. Ferries have neither: a crossing is on the water for its whole length.

Roads, railways, lifts, ferries, and trails all rise above the terrain by one print layer — 0.2 mm by default, settable from 0.08 to 0.4 mm. Set it to whatever layer height you slice at, so the line stays exactly one clean layer; the layer height section of the color guide covers why.

One Railway and lift history picker governs both of those layers, and shows whenever either is on.

SettingWhat it adds
Operational
the default		Running lines only.
DisusedTrack and lift lines still in place but out of use — rails, ties, ballast, cable, and pylons all still there.
AbandonedThose, plus lines whose rails have been lifted but whose formation still reads in the landscape: embankments, cuttings, a dead-straight trackbed, the cleared swath of an old lift line.

Out-of-service lines print thinner than running ones, and lifted formations thinner again — a scar, not a track. Lines tagged razed, dismantled, demolished, removed, or historic are never drawn at any setting, because nothing is left on the ground to print; neither are proposed or under-construction lines, because nothing is there yet.

Ferries have no history setting, because OpenStreetMap has no convention of disused ferry routes.

How much water to keep

A map of a river delta can hold more mapped stream than terrain. The waterway coverage cutoff sets how much of it prints: rivers and canals are always kept, then the longest streams are kept until their estimated printed area reaches the share you chose.

  • 0% — major waterways only.
  • 100% — every mapped stream.
  • Mapped water areas — lakes, reservoirs, wide rivers — ignore the cutoff entirely.

OpenStreetMap water can be switched off on its own without hiding the satellite-derived WorldCover water underneath it.

The sea, and where you put its surface

A real sea is level. The elevation source does not know that: it hands back whatever it holds under the water, which with Mapzen can be coarse seabed data. Printed, that becomes blue bathymetric relief — a bay with hills in it.

Sea surface, in the Water section, is the fix.

SettingWhat the sea does
Bathymetric relief
the default		 The source depths print as they arrive, draped like any other terrain. Existing models are unaffected.
Flat surface The water joined to the open sea at the map's edge is levelled to one plane. Lakes, reservoirs, and inland hollows keep their own heights.

The coastline is consulted before anything moves. Finding the sea by flooding inward from the map's edge is nearly right, and wrong in one memorable way: satellite water cannot tell the ocean from a below-sea-level inland basin that happens to reach the edge. The Salton Sea is 70 m below zero and not the ocean. So the mapped natural=coastline ways are assembled into the real sea outline first, and only border water inside it may start the flood. Where a map has no mapped coastline, the fill trusts the satellite water as it would otherwise.

Which water level

A coast has more than one defensible height, so the level is a choice of its own.

  • Mean sea level — the elevation source's own zero.
  • Low tide and high tide — the nearest NOAA tide station's published MLLW and MHHW, read as offsets from local mean sea level. That covers United States coasts; elsewhere they fall back to mean sea level and say so in the job's data sources, and a station far from the map carries its own caveat.
  • A custom offset in metres. Below zero dries out the foreshore between the two planes — the intertidal ground a low tide exposes. Above zero floods sea-connected land under it.

The level that was actually used, and the vertical reference it was measured against, are both recorded with the finished job.

Two things to know. Flattening needs color mode on, since it is the land-cover data that says which samples are water in the first place. And a super-tile shares one plane across every tile by construction — the level is worked out without reading any one tile's heights, so it cannot drift from tile to tile.

Buildings

Building mode raises OpenStreetMap footprints above the terrain, using each one's tagged height first, then its floor count, then an 8 m default. In color output, roofs and walls print in their own editable building material rather than inheriting the land-cover color under the footprint.

Its Z scale exaggerates that height against the map's plan scale, and the exaggeration eases back to true height as the ground span narrows. That is deliberate: the plan scale itself grows as you close in, so a multiplier that makes a 100 m tower a readable 5 mm across 18 km would make a 200 m tower 72 mm across 2.5 km — taller than the whole terrain relief. Close in, buildings are tall enough to read without help.

Trails you bring yourself

The Surface tab imports GPX and KML files. Each track, route, LineString, or gx:Track becomes one trail, named from the file and drawn as a raised line like a road.

  • Trails have their own color and their own width slider.
  • They show on the map preview and in the 3D legend.
  • They live in the model spec, so saved setups and exported setup files carry them.
  • Files are parsed in the browser. Tracks longer than 20,000 points are thinned on import, and a model holds up to 20 trails.

Markers

Markers are placed by latitude and longitude rather than drawn from map data, so they mark the things a map does not know about — where you camped, where the route crosses, whose house that is.
